Final Report from the Expenditures & Revenues Study Committee (E&RSC)

The committee's final report was released on March 13, 2026.

Brookline's Expenditures & Revenues Study Committee has now released its final report on the town's fiscal condition and recommendations for the Select Board as override discussions continue.

AI-generated summary of the report:

Brookline's Expenditures and Revenues Study Committee argues that the Town faces a structural mismatch between revenues and costs: Proposition 2 1/2 limits annual tax growth, while benefits, debt service, wages, and other expenses rise faster. Over the next three fiscal years, the report projects a $7.95 million structural deficit for Town government and a $17.94 million structural deficit for the Public Schools of Brookline. The committee therefore concludes that an override is likely necessary, but says this override is harder than past ones because of both the size of the ask and residents' existing tax and housing burdens.

The report lays out three ballot approaches. Option 1 would place the full $23.25 million combined request on the ballot: $5.31 million for Town services and $17.94 million for Schools. Option 2 would front-load levy growth in FY27, reducing the total override amount to $18.6 million by creating excess levy capacity early and using reserve/stabilization funds later. Option 3, the committee's preferred approach, would give voters a neutral tiered ballot rather than a binary yes-or-no choice. The committee says that giving voters meaningful intermediate options would maximize the chance of passage and better reflect the real tradeoffs facing residents.

A central reason for that preference is the report's community-impact analysis. It says Brookline's average single-family property taxes rose 93% from FY15 to FY26, versus a 64% median increase in comparable school communities, and that Brookline's increase was 146% of the comparable-community rate. It also finds that thousands of residents are already housing-burdened: over 3,300 owners pay more than 30% of household income on housing and about 2,800 pay more than 35%; among renters, over 6,650 pay more than 30% and over 5,300 more than 35%. Seniors are identified as especially burdened. The committee ties this data directly to its argument that voters should not be presented with a simple all-or-nothing override and that the full one-year and three-year tax impacts should be clearly disclosed.

The Schools Subcommittee supports an override only if the Town, School Committee, and PSB make a formal commitment to cost-control and management reforms, including reducing Brookline's unusually generous health-insurance contribution over time, containing wage growth, studying a revised school model, completing MUNIS integration, enforcing contractual teaching-load rules, reviewing technology leases and ed-tech subscriptions, requiring fair-market school-space rentals, piloting a Town-PSB facilities-governance model, and moving BEEP closer to full-cost recovery. It also highlights roughly $4 million in unreimbursed in-district special-education costs for 144 non-resident METCO and Materials Fee students, recommends a third-party CLA follow-up, and calls for outside demographic/enrollment forecasting every three years.

The Town Subcommittee separately backs a $5.31 million Town override to preserve services, meet public-safety obligations, stabilize fire overtime, restore six police positions, support tree-canopy and sustainability staffing, replace expiring federal IT funding, maintain senior transportation, and create a stabilization reserve; without an override, it warns of service cuts including possible temporary fire-company closure. The Future Subcommittee's clearest long-term recommendation is a $3.2 million solar initiative projected to supply about 13% of current electricity use and avoid about $390,000 a year, alongside additional electric demand-management work. Overall, the report's core message is that Brookline likely needs an override, but that the override should be designed around voters' actual capacity to pay and paired with clearer choices, cost controls, and longer-term structural reforms.
