Town Administrator's Budget 101

The Town Administrator's virtual Budget 101 session was held on February 26, 2026. The recording is now available.

The session explains how Brookline's municipal budget is built, what drives annual spending growth, and why residents are being asked to weigh difficult trade-offs about services and taxes.

A core theme was transparency: the town presented how decisions are made, how resources are divided between schools and municipal departments, and why long-term planning matters for both service levels and taxpayer burden.

Budget scale and structure: Brookline's combined town-and-schools operating budget is roughly $480-$500 million per year and typically grows around 3-4% annually. The presentation notes that most spending is already committed to ongoing services and personnel.

Major revenue sources discussed:

  • Property taxes (about 70-75% of revenue)
  • State aid (about 10-12%)
  • Local receipts such as permits, fees, and parking (about 10-15%)
  • Other sources, including enterprise funds and transfers

The presentation also reviewed Proposition 2 1/2 limits, under which levy growth is generally capped at 2.5% plus new growth. Because Brookline is relatively built out, new growth revenue is limited.

Where the money goes:

  • Public Schools of Brookline (roughly 50-55% of total spending)
  • Town departments, including police, fire, public works, parks, libraries, and administration
  • Benefits and pensions
  • Debt service and capital commitments

One of the key figures highlighted was that about 80-85% of costs are personnel-related (salary, health insurance, and pension obligations), which limits short-term budget flexibility.

Current financial pressures: The discussion identified rising health insurance costs, collective bargaining commitments, school expenses, transportation, special education costs, infrastructure needs, and funding uncertainty as major drivers of structural pressure.

A recurring point was the structural gap between typical revenue growth (around 3%) and expense growth (often 4-6%), which requires annual balancing through efficiencies, service reductions, reserve use, or potential new revenue.

School budget context presented: Recent planning references a projected school-side structural gap of roughly $8 million, driven by special education, enrollment, transportation, wage agreements, and benefit costs.

The session concluded by framing decisions in the next 1-3 budget cycles as especially important, with options including operational reforms, service reductions, override discussions, and broader long-term financial restructuring.
